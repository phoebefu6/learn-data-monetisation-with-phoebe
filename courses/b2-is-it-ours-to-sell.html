Part 1 · covers lawful basis and purpose

Collected for one purpose, sold for another

5 min live

A motor claim is collected to settle a claim. An index sold to fleet operators is a different purpose. Under the GDPR, every use of personal data needs one of six lawful bases in Article 6(1), and a new purpose that rests on neither fresh consent nor a law has to pass the compatibility test in Article 6(4). Singapore's PDPA asks the same question in its own words: consent, deemed consent, or an exception that actually covers what you plan to do.

LiveWhat the GDPR asks of a new purpose3 min▶

Six lawful bases. Article 6(1) lists them: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests. Settling a claim rests on the contract. Selling an index built from the claim does not, so the sale needs a basis of its own.

The compatibility test. Where the new purpose rests on neither the person's consent nor a law, Article 6(4) has the controller weigh the link between the old and new purpose, the context of collection, the nature of the data, including special categories, the possible consequences for the people, and the safeguards in place. Aggregation is a safeguard that helps; it does not answer the other four questions for you.

Consent, if you ask for it. Article 7(3) requires that consent be as easy to withdraw as to give. A product built on consent has to keep working when some customers withdraw, which means the share you can sell moves every quarter.

Self-studyThe same question under Singapore's PDPA3 min▶

Consent and deemed consent. Section 13 makes consent the default. Section 15A allows deemed consent by notification, but only after an assessment that the new use is not likely to have an adverse effect on the individual, and with a reasonable period to opt out.

Legitimate interests. The legitimate interests exception also needs an assessment of adverse effect before you rely on it.

Business improvement. The business improvement exception covers using data inside the organisation, and sharing it only within a group of related companies. A sale to an unrelated third party is not covered on the text. That is an inference from the wording, not a ruling, so treat it as a reason to ask counsel, not as a green light. The PDPC's Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts (revised 29 April 2026) set out these exceptions in full; PDPA & DNC ↗ teaches them operationally.

Real world

"Analytics to improve our services." Keelson's 2019 motor policy notice told customers their data would be used for "analytics to improve our services" and said nothing about third parties. Maya's team read it twice. Analytics inside Keelson: arguably covered. An index sold to a fleet operator: a different party and a different purpose, with no word in the notice pointing at it. The records sold under notices like that one are what the widget's first stage removes. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers objection, special category and the licence chain

Promises that travel with the data

5 min live

Three more checks sit between a lawful purpose and a sellable record. Customers may have objected. Some records carry health data. And some data arrived from a supplier under a contract that says what Keelson may do with it. Inbound terms bind outbound use: you cannot sell more than you were given.

LiveObjection, health data and the supplier contract3 min▶

The right to object. Under Article 21 of the GDPR, a person may object to processing that rests on public task or legitimate interests, bases (e) and (f). If Keelson relies on legitimate interests for analytics, the customers who objected come out of the product, and stay out.

Health data. Article 9(1) prohibits processing data concerning health unless one of the exceptions in Article 9(2) applies, such as explicit consent. Keelson's injury claims carry exactly that: notes about whiplash, hospital stays, recovery time. Selling an index is not one of the exceptions, so the injury claims are held back from the product entirely.

The licence chain. Repair garages supplied invoice lines to Keelson under a supply contract that limits their use to settling Keelson's own claims. Whatever Keelson's privacy notice says to customers, the garage's contract caps what Keelson may pass on. Records whose repair detail came under that contract cannot go into the index until the contract changes.

Self-studyReading an inbound contract before an outbound sale2 min▶

Four clauses decide most cases. Field of use: what the data may be used for, and whether "analytics" or "derived works" is named. Onward transfer: whether any third party may receive the data or anything derived from it. Derived data: who owns an aggregate built from the supplied records. Term and survival: whether the restriction outlives the contract. A data sharing register, owned by Data Governance ↗, is where those answers should already live. If nobody can find the contract, treat the data as restricted until someone does.

Real world

Terms settled before the data moved. Verisk describes ClaimSearch as a contributory, "give to get" claims database: insurers contribute their claims and in return may search the pool. Verisk states it holds over 1.8 billion US claims and covers about 95 percent of the US P&C market (Verisk blog, 31 July 2025; the vendor's own statement). The lesson for Keelson is the order of work: the contribution terms, who may use what and for what, were agreed before any claim moved. Keelson's garage contracts were written for settling claims, and nobody wrote an onward-use term because nobody planned to sell.

Part 3 · covers anonymous, pseudonymous and the sellable share

Anonymous is a high bar, and pseudonymous does not reach it

5 min live

The last check is about form. Data that can still be linked to a person is personal data, and every check above keeps applying to it. Only anonymous information sits outside the law, and getting there is harder than removing names. Keelson's index is published as aggregated cells, with small cells suppressed, which is one control on the road toward anonymous, not the arrival.

LivePseudonymised, anonymous and the threshold3 min▶

Pseudonymised is still personal. Article 4(5) of the GDPR defines pseudonymisation as processing so the data cannot be attributed to a person without additional information kept separately. Because that information exists somewhere, the data is still personal data, and every check in Parts 1 and 2 applies to it.

Anonymous is outside the law. Recital 26 puts anonymous information, data that no longer relates to an identifiable person, outside the Regulation. The PDPC's Advisory Guidelines on Selected Topics, Chapter 3 Anonymisation (revised 23 May 2024), take the same line for Singapore: anonymised data is not personal data.

A threshold is one control. Keelson suppresses any cell with fewer than k records, k 10 in the base case. That removes the cells where a single claim could be spotted, and it is one control among several; it does not by itself make data anonymous. Proving anonymity is the work of Privacy Engineering (planned), which owns the maths.

Keelson's result. After all five checks, 72.4 percent of the 180,000 records end in published cells, and 1,067 of 1,152 cells are published. The build-along takes that number apart.

Self-studyThe stakes, and the EU rules around data sharing3 min▶

The stakes. Article 83(5) of the GDPR allows fines of up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for infringements that include the basic principles and the conditions for consent. A data product priced in hundreds of thousands sits beside a penalty ceiling set by group turnover.

The EU Data Act. Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 applies from 12 September 2025, with some parts staggered into 2026 and 2027. Its Article 8 fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms, and Article 9 compensation that "may include a margin", apply only where a data holder is obliged to make data available. They are not a price rule for a voluntary sale like Keelson's index.

Data intermediaries. The Data Governance Act (2022/868) regulates data intermediation services from 24 September 2023, relevant if Keelson sold through, or became, a neutral intermediary. The Commission's Digital Omnibus (November 2025) proposes repealing the DGA into the Data Act: a proposal, not adopted as at October 2026.

A vocabulary. The ODI's Data Spectrum describes data running "from closed to shared to open". Keelson's index would be shared: licensed to named buyers on terms, not published for anyone.

Real world

"Just take the names off." One of the fleet operators asked Sefa for record-level claims "with the names taken off", so its own analysts could cut the data their way. Maya's answer was short: with policy numbers swapped for codes, every record is still personal data, every check applies to each one, and the injury notes come with them. The offer that went back was the aggregated index, with small cells suppressed. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Keelson wants to use claims data, collected to settle claims, for an index sold outside, without fresh consent and without a law requiring it. What does GDPR Article 6(4) ask?

Article 6(4) is the compatibility test for a new purpose that rests on neither consent nor a law. Aggregation counts as a safeguard inside the test; it does not skip it.

2 · A buyer asks for claims records with policy numbers replaced by codes. Why is that still personal data?

Pseudonymised data stays inside the law. Only anonymous information, where nobody can reasonably be identified, falls outside it under Recital 26.

3 · Why does Singapore's business improvement exception not cover selling the index to an unrelated fleet operator?

The wording limits sharing to related companies. No ruling is cited here; it is a reading of the text, and the right next step is counsel, not a sale.
