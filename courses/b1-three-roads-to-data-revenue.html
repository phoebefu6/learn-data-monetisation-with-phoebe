Three roads to data revenue

"Monetise our data" usually means "sell our data", and selling is the road that brings in the smallest share of what companies actually earn from data. MIT's Center for Information Systems Research found three roads: improving how work is done, wrapping data into existing products, and selling information offerings to others. Tonight: what each road is, where the returns came from in their research, what each road asks of the organisation, and a way to tell which road a particular dataset belongs on. Keelson Mutual is constructed.

Part 1 · covers improve, wrap and sell

Selling is one road of three, and the narrowest

5 min live

MIT CISR defines it plainly: "Data monetization is the act of turning data into money" (Wixom, Beath and Owens, 2023). Their book, Data Is Everybody's Business, names three ways: improving work, so processes run better or cheaper; wrapping products, adding data and analytics to something customers already buy; and selling information offerings, which CISR stresses is "not just about selling data; it's also about selling insights".

LiveThe three roads, in one line each3 min▶

Improving work. Data makes your own processes better: Keelson's claims fast-track triage, fraud scoring at first notice of loss. The money shows up as lower cost. Data Strategy owns this road: Data Strategy ↗.

Wrapping products. Data is added to something customers already buy, so they buy more, stay longer or pay more: a driving-score dashboard inside a fleet insurance policy. The money shows up as product revenue and retention.

Selling information offerings. Someone outside pays for the data or for insight built from it: an index, a report, an API feed, an analysis. The money shows up as a new revenue line, with new obligations attached.

CISR's 2023 release reported 51 percent of returns from improving, 31 percent from wrapping and 18 percent from selling. Those are shares of returns among the firms surveyed. They do not say selling is a bad idea; they say it is the road with the smallest share, and it is the one with a buyer, a contract and a regulator watching.

Self-studyDirect and indirect, the other way to slice it2 min▶

Doug Laney, author of Infonomics, splits monetisation into indirect and direct in his talks (2020). Indirect includes improving process performance, developing new solutions, building partner relationships and "publishing branded indices". Direct includes bartering or trading information, enhancing products with it, "selling raw data through brokers or data markets" and "offering insights, analyses and reports". Keelson's repair cost index sits interestingly on both lists: published freely it is a branded index that builds reputation; sold, it is a direct offering. That choice is part of tonight's build-along.

Real world

Choosing not to charge. Strava Metro, built from the activity data of Strava's users, has been offered free of charge to urban planners, city governments, transport departments and safe infrastructure advocates since September 2020 (reported). A company with saleable data chose a road where the return is goodwill, partnerships and better cycling infrastructure for its own users. Selling was available; it was not the only road.

Part 2 · covers the data monetisation capabilities

Every road runs on the same five capabilities

5 min live

CISR's research ties monetisation to a set of enterprise capabilities. As of its July 2026 briefing they are five for monetising data: data management, data platform, data science, customer understanding and acceptable data use, with a sixth, AI explanation, added for AI. The names have changed since CISR's earlier work, so quote the date with them.

LiveWhat selling asks of each capability3 min▶

Data management and data platform. A buyer expects the index on time, every quarter, with documented definitions. Internal users forgive a late refresh; a paying customer asks for a credit.

Data science. Building insight someone outside will trust, not only a model your own team understands.

Customer understanding. For selling, this means understanding a customer you have never served: what a fleet operator would do with repair cost data, and what that is worth to them. Analyst session 4 prices exactly this.

Acceptable data use. Whether the data may leave at all, in what form, and what the buyer may do with it. It is the subject of Leader session 2.

Self-studyHow much do high performers earn from data?2 min▶

CISR's briefings of November 2024 and July 2026, the latter based on a survey of 349 executives, report that top performers attribute 11 percent of revenues to data monetisation, more than five times the 2 percent of the bottom performers. It is self-reported, not audited, and it covers all three roads, not selling alone.

What you will not find is a reliable success rate for data monetisation programmes. A 2019 BARC survey sponsored by Tableau (about 200 respondents) found 17 percent of companies had established data monetisation initiatives, which measures adoption, not success. Figures quoted for how many such programmes "fail" could not be traced to a study. If a slide shows one, ask what it measured.

Part 3 · covers choosing the road for a dataset

Which road is this dataset on?

5 min live

A buyer on the phone makes selling feel like the obvious road. Three questions, asked in order, usually show whether it is. The first is about rights, the second about your own use, the third about whether you can run a product. Fail the first and the other two do not matter.

LiveAsking the three questions about the repair cost index3 min▶

1 · A lawful buyer? For aggregated motor claims, probably yes, for most records; Leader session 2 measures how many. For the repairer invoice lines, no: a supply contract limits them to settling Keelson's own claims.

2 · Would selling undercut our own use? Keelson prices motor policies partly on repair cost. Selling a regional repair cost index to smaller insurers hands competitors part of that edge. Selling it to fleet operators and repair networks does not. The answer depends on the buyer, which is why Analyst session 4 prices segments separately.

3 · Can Keelson run a product? Not yet: there is no refresh process a customer could rely on, no support, and no contract template. That points either to building those (Analyst session 3 costs them) or to a partner who already has them (Analyst session 5).

Self-studyThree lenses on what a dataset is worth2 min▶

Before choosing a road, it helps to know what the dataset is worth on each. Analyst session 1 computes three lenses on Keelson's five assets: what each cost to build and keep, what lawful buyers would pay, and what it earns in use. The repairer invoice lines show why the order of the three questions matters: 1200.0k in use, 0.0k on the market. The motor claims history is the only asset that ranks first on all three, which is why the index is built on it. The widget in the build-along below shows the same numbers.

Real world

The second question, asked late. Two of the three fleet operators who called Sefa turned out to be insurers' fleet arms. Tom Reyes, the COO, asked whether a regional repair cost index sold to them would help them undercut Keelson on fleet policies. The answer reshaped the buyer list before anyone discussed price. Homework

Before Leader session 2

30 min

  2. Find your privacy notice. For your candidate dataset, find the notice it was collected under and copy what it says about other uses.
  3. Ask about the capabilities. Which of the five capabilities is weakest in your organisation for a product sold outside? Ask one colleague who would know.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · MIT CISR reported 51, 31 and 18 percent for improving, wrapping and selling. What are those percentages of?

They are shares of returns. Quoting them as revenue, or as a share of firms, changes what they claim.

2 · Which question should be asked first about a dataset a buyer wants?

If nobody may lawfully buy it, the other questions do not arise. Keelson's invoice lines stop here.

3 · A slide says "most data monetisation programmes fail". What is the honest response?

CISR's 18 percent is a share of returns; BARC's 17 percent is adoption. Neither is a failure rate.
