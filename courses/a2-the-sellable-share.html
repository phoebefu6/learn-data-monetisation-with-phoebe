The sellable share

Owning a dataset and being free to sell it are two different things. Every record in Keelson Mutual's motor claims history has to pass four checks before it may go into a product: the privacy notice it was collected under, the customer's own objection, the licence terms it arrived with, and whether it carries health data. What survives is then cut into cells, and any cell too small to publish safely is dropped. Tonight: the funnel built stage by stage, the cell threshold computed on the grid, and the honest limit of what a threshold does. Keelson is constructed; the arithmetic runs in your browser.

Part 1 · covers the rights funnel

Every record has to pass four checks

5 min live

Picture the 180,000 records walking through four gates in a row. At each gate some leave, and the next gate only sees the ones that are left. Keelson's constructed shares: the privacy notice did not cover reuse for 8%, customers objected for 3%, a third-party licence forbids onward use for 12%, and injury claims, which carry health data, are held back at 7%. The licence gate removes the most records, 19,276.

LiveWhy each gate exists3 min▶

Gate 1 · the notice did not cover reuse. Data collected to settle a claim was collected for that purpose. Under the GDPR, Article 6(4) asks whether a new purpose is compatible with the original one, weighing the link between the purposes, the context of collection, the nature of the data (including whether it is special category), the possible consequences for the people involved and the safeguards in place. In Singapore the PDPA starts from consent (section 13), and section 15A allows deemed consent by notification only after an assessment of adverse effects and a reasonable period to opt out. Records collected under a notice that never mentioned an outside product fail here.

Gate 2 · the customer objected. GDPR Article 21 gives people a right to object to processing that rests on public task or legitimate interests (bases (e) and (f) of Article 6(1)). A customer who objected to analytics use is out, whatever the business case says.

Gate 3 · a third-party licence forbids onward use. This one is contract, not statute. Some records carry fields Keelson received from others under terms that limit how they may be used. Inbound terms bind outbound use: Keelson cannot sell what it was only licensed to use.

Gate 4 · injury claims carry health data. GDPR Article 9(1) prohibits processing data concerning health unless one of the Article 9(2) exceptions applies. Keelson holds injury claims back as a class rather than argue an exception for a commercial product.

Self-studyOrder matters, and shares compound2 min▶

Each share is measured on the records that reached that gate, not on the original 180,000. So the licence gate removes 12% of 160,632, which is 19,276, not 12% of 180,000, which would be 21,600. Change the order of the gates and the count credited to each gate changes, which is why a funnel always states its order.

The same logic is why you never add the shares. 8% + 3% + 12% + 7% = 30%, and 180,000 x 70% = 126,000. The true remainder is 131,461, because each later share bites on a smaller pile. Adding the shares understates the eligible records by 5,461, and it hides the fact that a point saved at a late gate is worth fewer records than a point saved at an early one.

The order Keelson uses follows the questions a reviewer would ask: is there any lawful basis for the new purpose, has this person said no, do we hold the right to pass it on, and is it a category the law treats separately.

Self-studyThe PDPA exception that does not stretch to a sale2 min▶

Singapore's PDPA has a business improvement exception that lets an organisation use personal data without consent for purposes such as improving its own products and services. On the text, it covers use within the organisation and sharing within a group of related companies. A sale to an unrelated buyer is not covered on the text; that is an inference from the wording, and it is the reading to take until the PDPC says otherwise. The legitimate interests exception exists too, but it needs an assessment of adverse effects before it can be relied on (PDPC Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts, revised 29 April 2026).

The operational detail of consent, notification and Do Not Call lives in PDPA and Do Not Call ↗; who owns the decision lives in Data Governance ↗. Here the only question is what makes a record sellable.

Real world

The 2019 notice. When Maya's team pulled the privacy notices behind the motor book, the policies sold before Keelson's 2019 notice update said the data would be used "to administer your policy and claims". Nothing about statistics, nothing about anyone outside Keelson. Ines Carvalho, the CFO, asked whether a quick re-notification would fix it. Maya's answer: deemed consent by notification needs an adverse-effect assessment and a fair opt-out window first, and it only covers records going forward from a defensible process. Those records sit at gate 1 until that work is done. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers the k threshold

Small cells point at people

5 min live

The index never ships records. The 131,461 eligible records are counted into cells: 12 regions x 8 vehicle classes x 12 quarters = 1,152 cells. A cell with fewer than k records is suppressed, meaning it is left blank in the product. At k 10, 85 cells are suppressed and 1,067 of 1,152 are published (92.6%). The suppressed cells hold 584 records, and 130,304 records sit in published cells: a sellable share of 72.4% of the original 180,000.

LiveWhy a small cell is a re-identification risk3 min▶

An index cell looks harmless: an average repair cost and a claim count for one region, one vehicle class, one quarter. But if that cell holds two claims, and one of them is a rare vehicle in a small region, a neighbour, a repairer or a fleet manager who knows about one accident can read the other's repair cost straight off the published number. The fewer records in a cell, the closer the cell is to describing a person.

A minimum cell size, written k, is the simplest guard: publish a cell only if at least k records fall into it. Keelson's base is k 10. The rule is easy to explain to a buyer, easy to test and easy to audit, which is why it is a common first control on any aggregated product.

Self-studyWhat the widget actually counts2 min▶

The engine spreads the eligible records across the 1,152 cells using fixed weights, set by a seeded draw so every run builds the same grid: big regions and common vehicle classes get many records, small regions and rare classes get few. Each cell's count is rounded down to whole records. Any cell under k is suppressed and its records are counted as lost.

Three numbers come out. Cells published is the share of the 1,152 cells that clear k. Records lost to suppressed cells is the count sitting in the blanks. Sellable share is records in published cells over the original 180,000: 130,304 / 180,000 = 72.4%. Notice the denominator: the sellable share is measured against everything Keelson holds, so it carries the four rights gates and the threshold together.

Real world

Why pooled claims databases exist. Verisk describes its ClaimSearch service as a contributory "give to get" claims database holding over 1.8 billion US claims, about 95 percent of the US property and casualty market by its own account (Verisk, July 2025). A pool that size fills cells that a single insurer's book leaves thin. Keelson's index is one insurer's claims, so its small regions and rare vehicles will always sit near the threshold; that is a limit of the product, worth saying to buyers before they find it.

Part 3 · covers cells against records, and what a threshold is not

Cells fall fast, records fall slowly

5 min live

Raise k and two shares move at very different speeds. From k 10 to k 50, cells published fall from 92.6% to 50.1%, a loss of 42.5 points, while records in published cells fall from 72.4% to 64.9%, only 7.5 points. The cells that vanish are the small regions and rare vehicles: few records, but exactly the detail a buyer pays for.

LiveWhy the two shares part3 min▶

Records are piled unevenly. A big region with common vehicles fills a cell with hundreds of records; a small region with a rare class fills one with a handful. Raising k first removes cells that hold almost nothing, so the cell count drops quickly while the record count barely notices. Only at high k does the threshold start to bite into cells that held real volume.

The commercial point: a buyer does not pay for records, it pays for answers. A repair network in a small region wants that region's cells; a parts maker wants the rare vehicle classes. Those are the cells that go first. Analyst session 6 prices the index in proportion to cells published, which is how a stricter threshold reaches revenue: at the tightened rung, cells published 68.7% / 92.6% = 74.1% of the base price, so 32.0k behaves like 23.7k.

Self-studyWhat a threshold is not2 min▶

Pseudonymised is still personal. Replacing names and policy numbers with codes is pseudonymisation, and GDPR Article 4(5) keeps pseudonymised data inside the definition of personal data, because the codes can be linked back with the key.

Anonymous is outside the law, and hard to reach. GDPR Recital 26 places anonymous information outside the Regulation, and the PDPC's Advisory Guidelines on Selected Topics, Chapter 3 on anonymisation (revised 23 May 2024), say anonymised data is not personal data. Both turn on whether a person can still be identified, by any means reasonably likely to be used.

A cell threshold is one control, never anonymisation on its own. It does not stop someone comparing two quarters to isolate a change, joining the index to another source, or reading a rare cell that cleared k by one record. Whether a published product is anonymous depends on the whole set of controls and the context it is released into. The maths of k-anonymity and differential privacy belongs to Privacy Engineering ↗; here k is a product control with a known cost.

Real world

The detail the fleet buyer wanted. In the pricing workshop Sefa asked for k 5: a fleet operator had said the small regions were the reason to buy. Maya ran the widget in the room. k 5 publishes 99.0% of cells against 92.6% at k 10, and gains only 0.3 points of records (72.7% against 72.4%). The cells it adds are the thinnest, rarest ones, the very cells the threshold exists to hold back. The team kept k 10 and wrote the limit into the product sheet instead. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Keelson's four gates remove 8%, 3%, 12% and 7%. Why do 131,461 records remain rather than 126,000?

The licence gate takes 12% of 160,632, not of 180,000. Adding the four shares to 30% overstates the loss; compounding them is the right arithmetic, and the order decides how much each gate is credited with.

2 · Raising k from 10 to 50 cuts cells published by 42.5 points but records in published cells by only 7.5. Why?

Small regions and rare vehicles hold a handful of records each. Dropping them costs little volume and a lot of detail, which is the detail buyers want.

3 · A colleague says the index is anonymous because every published cell holds at least 10 records. What is the accurate reply?

GDPR Article 4(5) keeps pseudonymised data personal, and Recital 26 and PDPC Chapter 3 turn on whether anyone can still be identified. No single k settles that.
