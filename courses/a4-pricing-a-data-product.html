Pricing a data product

A data product costs almost nothing to copy one more time, so its cost tells you very little about its price. What sets the price is the decision the data improves for the buyer, and how much of that improvement the buyer will hand over. Tonight: willingness to pay built from value and a capture share, a search for the best single price across 110 constructed prospects, a hard look at who actually buys at that price, and what a second tier adds. Keelson is constructed; the arithmetic runs in your browser.

Part 1 · covers value to the buyer and capture share

Price the decision it improves, not the rows

5 min live

A buyer does not want rows. A fleet operator wants to set its repair budget by region with fewer surprises; a parts maker wants to know where to stock. Each prospect gets a yearly value from the index, and pays at most a share of it. Keelson's constructed assumption is a capture share of 25 percent: a buyer pays at most a quarter of the value it gets. Willingness to pay is value x capture share. Sort the 110 prospects by it and a price becomes a horizontal line.

LiveValue to the buyer, then the capture share3 min▶

Start with the decision. For each kind of buyer, name the decision the index improves and what a better decision is worth to them in a year. In Keelson's constructed prospect list, fleet operators value it between 10k and 90k a year, repair networks between 20k and 160k, parts makers between 60k and 320k, and smaller insurers between 40k and 240k. The values are seeded and skewed low: most prospects sit near the bottom of their range.

Then the capture share. No buyer pays all the value it gets; if it did, buying would leave it no better off. The capture share is the slice you can ask for. Keelson uses 25 percent as a constructed assumption, and the widget puts it on a slider because it is the least certain number on the page.

Willingness to pay = value x capture share. A parts maker pays at most a quarter of whatever the index is worth to it in a year. Sorted from highest to lowest, the 110 prospects make the staircase in the figure.

Self-studyWhy volume is the wrong unit2 min▶

Pricing per row, per record or per gigabyte feels fair because it is countable. It fails for two reasons. First, the cost of one more row is close to zero, so a per-row price has nothing underneath it. Second, the value to the buyer does not scale with rows: a fleet operator with ten vans in one region gets as much from the regional index as one with ten thousand rows of raw claims it would have to clean itself. The CDAIO data monetisation module puts it plainly: price by the decision the data enables, not by the volume delivered.

Volume still matters as a fence. A buyer who wants every region at monthly grain gets more decisions out of the data than one who wants a single region quarterly, and the price can follow that. That is a tier, which is Part 3.

Real world

The first quote was per row. Sefa's first draft quote for a fleet operator priced the index by the number of claims records behind each regional figure. The operator's buyer asked why a quieter region cost less when it was the region they were expanding into. Sefa rewrote the quote around the operator's repair budget instead, and the conversation moved from counting records to what a better budget saves. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers the single-price search

One price, and the buyers it picks for you

5 min live

With one product, the API feed, the search is simple: at each price count the buyers whose willingness to pay clears it, then contribution = buyers x (price - cost to serve). The API costs 9.0k a year to serve per customer. Too high and you sell to a handful; too low and each sale earns little. The best single price prints as 32.0k: 18 buyers, revenue 576.0k, contribution 414.0k.

LiveReading the hump3 min▶

At 32.0k: 18 buyers, revenue 18 x 32.0k = 576.0k, contribution 18 x (32.0k - 9.0k) = 414.0k.

At 40.0k: 10 buyers, contribution 310.0k. Each sale earns more, but eight buyers drop away.

At 24.0k: 22 buyers, contribution 330.0k. Four more buyers do not make up for 8.0k less on each of the eighteen who would have paid more.

The hump is flat near the top and steep on both sides, which is normal: small moves near the best price cost little, big moves cost a lot. Contribution here is before Keelson's shared fixed cost; Analyst session 3 put that at 256.7k a year, and the P&L desk in Analyst session 6 adds it back.

LiveThe buyer list is a strategy question2 min▶

At 32.0k the buyers by segment are fleet operators 0 of 60, repair networks 4 of 25, parts makers 6 of 10, smaller insurers 8 of 15. The largest group is Keelson's competitors. Leader session 1 asked this as its second question: would selling undercut our own use? Keelson prices motor policies partly on repair cost, and a regional repair cost index in a smaller insurer's hands narrows that edge.

The revenue-maximising price did not ask whether a buyer should be a customer. That is a separate decision, and it belongs to the person who owns the strategy, not to the price search. Options include excluding a segment, delaying its access, or selling it a coarser product. Each one moves the numbers; run the widget again after you decide.

Self-studyWhat this search leaves out2 min▶

This is business-to-business data pricing: a few dozen named prospects, each with a value you can reason about. Consumer price elasticity and price tests on retail goods are a different discipline, with thousands of buyers and experiments to measure response; that belongs to Pricing Analytics (planned). The search also treats every prospect as independent. In practice one buyer's decision moves another's: if two repair networks buy, a third may feel it has to.

Real world

Tom reads the list, not the total. When the single-price result went round, Tom Reyes, the COO, skipped the 414.0k and went straight to the segment table. "Eight of eighteen are people we compete with for fleet business." Maya Okafor asked for the same search with a second, coarser tier before anyone discussed excluding insurers. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 3 · covers two tiers

Two tiers, and who each one brings in

5 min live

A single price leaves money on both sides: buyers who would pay more pay the same as everyone, and buyers who would pay something pay nothing. A second tier fixes part of both. Keelson's Standard tier is the quarterly report, worth half the value of the full index and costing 1.5k a year to serve; Pro is the API at full value, costing 9.0k. Each buyer takes the tier with the larger surplus, meaning willingness to pay minus price. The best pair prints as Standard 9.0k + Pro 25.0k.

LiveWhat the second tier adds3 min▶

Twice the buyers. 18 Standard + 18 Pro = 36 buyers, against 18 at one price.

A little more contribution. Revenue 18 x 9.0k + 18 x 25.0k = 162.0k + 450.0k = 612.0k. Contribution 18 x (9.0k - 1.5k) + 18 x (25.0k - 9.0k) = 135.0k + 288.0k = 423.0k, against 414.0k for the single price: 9.0k more.

A different customer base. By segment (Standard / Pro / neither): fleet operators 8 / 0 / 52, repair networks 5 / 4 / 16, parts makers 4 / 6 / 0, smaller insurers 1 / 8 / 6. The Standard tier is what brings fleet operators in at all. The Pro price fell from 32.0k to 25.0k because a high Pro price would push Pro buyers down into Standard; the tiers have to be priced together.

The 9.0k is small, and that is honest. The bigger gain is strategic: the product now reaches the buyers Keelson would rather serve, which changes the answer to Part 2's buyer-list question.

Self-studyThe pricing families in the literature2 min▶

Jian Pei's survey, "A Survey on Data Pricing: From Economics to Data Science" (IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, 2022, online 2020), starts from the economics: information goods have "zero marginal costs" and are non-rival, so one buyer's use does not use the data up. The pricing models it reviews include versioning, bundling, subscription, auctions and query-based pricing. Keelson's Standard and Pro are versioning: the same underlying index offered at two levels of detail and delivery. Selling it by the year is subscription.

The point that a data product carries a large fixed cost and a small cost per extra customer is ours, from the Analyst session 3 cost curve, not Pei's.

Self-studyCost-plus as a floor and a check2 min▶

Analyst session 3 printed a cost-plus price of 31.1k: 23.9k per customer at 16 customers, plus a 30 percent markup. Tonight's value price is 32.0k. The two land close together here by coincidence, not by method. Change the capture share or the customer count and they part company.

Use cost-plus two ways. As a floor: a price below cost per customer at a realistic customer count loses money however good the story. As a check: if the value price sits far above cost-plus, ask whether the value estimates are too generous; if far below, ask whether the product is too expensive to build for this market.

Real world

Choosing a price of zero for one segment. Strava Metro, the activity-data product for transport planning, has been free of charge to urban planners, city governments, transport departments and safe infrastructure advocates since September 2020 (reported, Bicycle Retailer). Whatever the reasons, it is a segment decision made on purpose: the price for one group of buyers was set by what the company wanted from that group, not by a search for maximum contribution.

Homework

Before Analyst session 5

30 min
  1. Interview one buyer. Ask one real or likely buyer of your data what decision it would change and what a better version of that decision is worth to them in a year. Write their words, not your estimate.
  2. Pick a capture share and defend it. Write the share you would assume and one reason it might be too high. Then run your price worksheet at half and double that share.
  3. Read your buyer list. At your best single price, list who buys. Mark any buyer you compete with, and write what you would do about them.
  4. Design a second tier. Name what the cheaper tier leaves out, what it costs to serve, and which segment it is meant to bring in.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why price a data product by the value of the decision it improves rather than by the volume of records delivered?

With near-zero cost per extra copy, the only solid anchor for price is what the data does for the buyer. A small region can be worth more to a buyer expanding into it than a large one.

2 · At the best single price of 32.0k, 8 of Keelson's 18 buyers are smaller insurers. Why is that a problem the price search cannot solve?

The search maximises contribution. It never asks whether a buyer should be served. Leader session 1's second question, would selling undercut our own use, has to be asked separately.

3 · Keelson adds a Standard tier at 9.0k beside a Pro tier at 25.0k. What does the second tier add here?

The money gain is modest; the reach is not. Standard is what brings fleet operators in at all, which changes who the product serves.
