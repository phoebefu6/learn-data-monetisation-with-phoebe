Package and cost to serve

Part 1 · covers packaging tiers

Four ways to hand over the same index

5 min live

Packaging is the decision about what the buyer actually receives. The index numbers are the same in every tier; what changes is how they arrive, how much each format costs to build once, and how much it costs to keep one customer served for a year. Underneath every tier sits the same platform: the anonymisation pipeline, a data steward, the data protection impact assessment and the legal review. No customer buys the platform, and every customer pays for it.

LiveFour tiers, two numbers each3 min▶

Build is what it costs once to make the format exist: the report template and its checks (40.0k), the dashboard (90.0k), the API with its access control and uptime promise (160.0k). Bespoke analysis has no build cost, because every piece of it is made to order.

Cost to serve is what one customer costs for a year once the format exists: 1.5k to send a quarterly report and answer its questions, 4.0k for a dashboard seat and its support, 9.0k for an API customer's onboarding, monitoring and support, 35.0k of analyst time for a bespoke piece.

The shared platform is built once for 120.0k and runs at 150.0k a year. It is the reason the index can be sold at all: the pipeline applies the rights checks and cell suppression from Analyst session 2, the steward owns quality, and the DPIA and legal review keep it lawful.

Keelson's launch mix: half the customers take the report, 40% the API, 10% bespoke analysis. The dashboard is not offered at launch, so its 90.0k is not spent.

Self-studyWhich format fits which buyer2 min▶

CDAIO module 5 (data monetisation execution) sets out six delivery formats and who each suits. A dashboard or report suits non-technical stakeholders and is the least complex to run. A shared database view suits analysts with SQL skills. An API endpoint suits developers and applications. File delivery suits batch consumers and partners. Embedded analytics suits product teams and is complex. A data marketplace listing suits external monetisation and is also complex.

The same module's advice for a first release: do not over-engineer it, ship a CSV before building an API. For Keelson that points at the quarterly report with its index table as a file: the cheapest tier to build, the cheapest to serve, and the one that tells you whether buyers come back for the second quarter before 160.0k goes into an API.

A tour of data product types in their own right, beyond the selling decision, lives in Data Products ↗.

Real world

The API that waited a quarter. Sefa came to the packaging meeting with two repair networks asking for a live feed and argued for building the API first: "that is what serious buyers want". Maya Okafor, the CDO, put the tier table on the screen. The API costs 160.0k to build and 9.0k a year to serve each customer; the report costs 40.0k and 1.5k. She argued for the quarterly report first, so that the first paying customers would prove the index was worth repeat business before the larger build. The plan that went to Ines ships the report in the first quarter and keeps the API in the launch mix for the buyers who need a feed. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 2 · covers fixed against variable cost

The cost that stays and the cost that grows

5 min live

Split every cost by one question: does it change when one more customer signs? The platform run, the steward and the builds do not. The report mailing, the API support and the analyst's hours do. The first pile is the shared fixed cost, and it is large. The second is the cost to serve one more customer, and for most of a data product it is small, because a copy of an index costs almost nothing to make.

LiveTwo numbers, shown by hand3 min▶

Shared fixed cost per year. The platform runs at 150.0k a year. The builds are spent once, so spread them over three years: the platform 120 / 3 = 40.0, the report 40 and the API 160 together (40 + 160) / 3 = 66.7. The dashboard is not offered and bespoke has no build. So 150 + 40.0 + 66.7 = 256.7k a year, the same as 150 + (120 + 40 + 160) / 3.

Cost to serve one more customer. A new customer is, on average, half a report customer, four tenths of an API customer and one tenth of a bespoke one: 50% x 1.5 + 40% x 9 + 10% x 35 = 0.75 + 3.6 + 3.5 = 7.85, shown on the widget as 7.9k.

The first number is shared by however many customers there are. The second is paid again for every one of them.

Self-studyZero marginal cost, and the one tier that breaks it2 min▶

Jian Pei's survey of data pricing (IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, 2022) describes information goods as having "zero marginal costs" and as non-rival: one buyer reading the index does not use it up for the next. That is why Keelson's report tier costs only 1.5k a year per customer, and most of that is people answering questions, not the copy.

The high fixed cost that comes with it is a point about Keelson's own cost structure, read off tonight's numbers, not something to attribute to Pei. The pipeline, steward and reviews must exist before the first sale, and they do not shrink when sales are slow.

Bespoke analysis is the exception. It is labour, not copies: every bespoke customer needs analyst hours, so its cost to serve is 35.0k and it scales one to one with customers. One customer in ten on bespoke adds 3.5 of the 7.85, nearly half the variable cost. A product that drifts towards bespoke work stops behaving like a data product and starts behaving like a consultancy.

Self-studyWhy gross margin flatters a data product2 min▶

Gross margin here is revenue minus the cost to serve, over revenue. It leaves the shared fixed cost out, and for a data product the shared fixed cost is most of the cost. So the margin looks excellent while the product loses money. The P&L desk in Analyst session 6 shows exactly this: in Keelson's base case the gross margin is 72.5% in every year, while cumulative cash stays below zero until year 5 (-374.3k after year 1, +40.3k after year 5). Quote gross margin next to the fixed cost, never alone.

Real world

"It is a file, it costs nothing." When Ines Carvalho asked what one more customer would cost, the first answer from the platform team was "almost nothing, we just send another copy". The tier table said 7.9k, because one customer in ten buys bespoke analysis at 35.0k of analyst time and four in ten need API support at 9.0k. Ines asked for the bespoke share to be tracked every quarter as its own line. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Part 3 · covers the cost curve and cost-plus as a floor

A curve that falls, and a floor under the price

5 min live

Divide the fixed 256.7k by the number of customers, add the 7.9k, and you get the cost per customer. With 5 customers it is 59.2k. With 80 it is 11.1k. The curve falls steeply at first and then flattens, and it never drops below 7.9k, because that part is paid again for every customer however many there are.

LiveRead the curve, then put a floor under the price3 min▶

Steep at the start. Going from 5 to 10 customers takes 25.7k off each customer's cost (59.2k to 33.5k). Going from 40 to 80 takes off 3.2k (14.3k to 11.1k). Early customers carry the platform; late customers mostly carry their own serve cost.

Cost-plus. At 16 customers the cost per customer is 23.9k. Add a 30% markup and the cost-plus price is 31.1k (23.9 x 1.3 = 31.07, from the unrounded 23.89).

A floor and a check, never the price. In Analyst session 4 the value-based price for the API comes out at 32.0k. That cost-plus lands next to it is a coincidence of these constructed numbers, not a method. CDAIO module 5 puts the rule plainly: do not price based on cost, price based on customer value. Use cost-plus to check that a value price covers the cost at a customer count you believe, and use 7.9k as the line no single customer's price should go below.

Self-studyThe circularity trap2 min▶

Cost per customer depends on how many customers there are. How many customers there are depends on the price. So a cost-plus price depends on itself. Raise the price to cover a 59.2k cost at 5 customers and fewer buyers sign, which pushes the cost per customer up again. Lower it assuming 80 customers and you are pricing at 11.1k for a market you have not won.

The way out is to set the price from value (Analyst session 4), read off how many buyers that price wins, and only then read the cost curve at that count. The curve is the check, never the starting point.

Consumer price elasticity and price testing on retail goods belong to Pricing Analytics (planned). Business-to-business data pricing, by value to the buyer, is Analyst session 4.

Real world

Pricing below the floor on purpose. Strava Metro has been free of charge to urban planners, city governments, transport departments and safe infrastructure advocates since September 2020 (reported, Bicycle Retailer). Serving those users still costs something, so a zero price sits below any cost-to-serve floor. Strava's reasons are not set out in the sources we checked; the lesson is only that going below the floor is a decision a firm takes deliberately and pays for elsewhere, never one it drifts into because the cost curve was read at an optimistic customer count.

Homework

Before Analyst session 4

30 min
  1. Split one real cost line. Take the budget for one data service you run and mark each line fixed (does not move with one more user) or variable (does). Write the share that is fixed.
  2. Find your bespoke tier. Name the requests that arrive as "can you just pull this for us". Estimate the hours one of them takes; that is labour, not copies.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does gross margin flatter a data product like the Keelson index?

The serve cost is small and the platform is large. A margin that ignores the platform looks healthy while cash stays negative, as the desk's 72.5% gross margin beside a negative cumulative cash shows.

2 · Cost-plus at 16 customers gives 31.1k. Why is that a floor and a check, not the price?

Price on value, not cost. Cost-plus checks that a value price covers the cost at a believable customer count; it landing near 32.0k here is a coincidence.

3 · Keelson's customers double from 20 to 40. What happens to the cost per customer?

256.7k / 20 = 12.8k and 256.7k / 40 = 6.4k, each plus 7.9k. Only the fixed share is divided; the serve cost is paid again for every customer.
