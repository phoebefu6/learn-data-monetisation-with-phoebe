Three prices for one dataset

Ask three people what a dataset is worth and you get three answers: what it cost to build, what someone outside would pay for it, and what it earns when the business uses it. All three are legitimate, they rarely agree, and the gap between them is where a data monetisation conversation goes wrong. Tonight: an inventory of what Keelson Mutual holds, the three lenses computed on five of its assets, why they rank the assets differently, and the one fact about accounting every data leader gets asked. Keelson is constructed; the arithmetic runs in your browser.

Part 1 · covers the data asset inventory

Know what you hold, and who else has a say

5 min live

A data asset inventory is a list of datasets with the facts needed to value them: what it costs to collect and keep, how long it has been held, what the business uses it for, and who else has rights over it. The last column is the one most inventories leave out, and it decides whether the asset has a market at all.

LiveThe six columns every inventory row needs3 min▶

What it costs to collect and keep, per year, and how long it has been held. Together they give the cost lens.

Who may lawfully buy it, and at what yearly fee. Together they give the market lens. "May lawfully" carries the weight: the answer depends on the privacy notice the data was collected under, the contracts it arrived with and the law. Leader session 2 and Analyst session 2 go deep on that.

What it earns in use per year, what it costs to run, and for how many more years it stays useful. Together they give the economic lens.

Keelson's five rows are in the build-along widget. Your own inventory will be longer and rougher; a row with a guess in it is still better than a dataset nobody listed.

Self-studyWhy the rights column comes first2 min▶

A dataset can arrive with somebody else's terms attached. Keelson's repairer invoice lines come from partner garages under a supply contract that limits their use to settling Keelson's own claims: the right to use them is Keelson's, the right to pass them on is not. The claims call transcripts were recorded under a notice that mentioned quality and training, nothing about anyone outside Keelson. Neither has a market until those terms change, and changing them has its own cost. An inventory that lists those two assets next to a market price invites a decision that cannot be carried out.

Real world

The industry already pools claims data. Verisk describes its ClaimSearch service as a contributory "give to get" claims database holding over 1.8 billion US claims, about 95 percent of the US property and casualty market by its own account (Verisk, July 2025). Insurers contribute their claims and get matches back. That is data sold and shared at scale in exactly Keelson's industry, and it works because contribution terms were settled before any data moved.

Part 2 · covers the cost, market and economic lenses

Three lenses, three questions

5 min live

Each lens answers a different question, which is why none of them is wrong. The cost lens asks what it would take to have this data again. The market lens asks what buyers outside would pay. The economic lens asks what it earns when the business uses it. Singapore's Trusted Data Sharing Framework (IMDA and PDPC, 2019) names the same three approaches as cost, market and income.

LiveThe three formulas on the widget3 min▶

Cost lens = yearly cost to collect and keep x years held. A floor for what it would take to rebuild the asset, and the number auditors and insurers reach for. It says nothing about whether anyone wants the data.

Market lens = lawful buyers x yearly licence fee. One year of licence income at a market price. It is zero for an asset nobody may buy, however useful.

Economic lens = (yearly benefit in use - yearly run cost) x years of useful life. What the asset contributes to the business. Here it is undiscounted on purpose; Business Cases & ROI shows how to discount a stream of benefits, Analyst session 2 ↗.

These are teaching versions, kept simple so the arithmetic fits on one line. A real valuation adds discounting, the share of a benefit truly due to the data, and the decay of old records.

Self-studyLaney's six models, the fuller set2 min▶

Doug Laney's Infonomics sets out six ways to value information. In his own talks (TDWI keynote 2020; US Department of Energy talk, July 2020) he groups them as three foundational measures, the intrinsic, business and performance value of information (IVI, BVI, PVI), and three financial measures, the cost, market and economic value of information (CVI, MVI, EVI). His slides pose each as a question, for example how correct, complete and scarce the data is. The three lenses on tonight's widget borrow the names of the financial three; they are simplified teaching formulas, not Laney's published ones, which sit in the book and in paywalled Gartner research.

The foundational measures matter even when you never sell: an asset scoring poorly on correctness or completeness will disappoint any buyer, and Analyst session 2 shows how much of an asset survives the rights checks before quality is even asked.

Part 3 · covers why the lenses disagree

Same five assets, three rankings

5 min live

Put all five assets through all three lenses and rank them. Only the motor claims history comes first every time. The repairer invoice lines are second most valuable in use (1200.0k) and last on the market (0.0k). The telematics pilot cost 440.0k to build and earns 15.0k in use. A team that picks the asset to sell by its cost picks wrong; a team that picks by its use value can pick something it may not sell.

LiveWhich lens answers which question3 min▶

"Should we sell it?" starts with the market lens: no lawful buyer, no sale, whatever the other two say. Then compare it with the economic lens: if using the data earns more than selling it, selling a copy is only worth it when it does not undercut that use.

"Should we keep collecting it?" compares the cost lens with the economic lens. The telematics pilot costs 220.0k a year and earns 40.0k in use: the market lens is a distraction there.

"What do we tell the board it is worth?" Give all three, with the arithmetic, and say which question each answers. A single number invites the wrong decision.

Self-studyWhy none of this is on the balance sheet2 min▶

Under IFRS, IAS 38 paragraph 63 says internally generated brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists "and items similar in substance" shall not be recognised as intangible assets, and paragraph 57 sets six conditions before development spending can become one. The standard never mentions data or databases. Treating a home-grown dataset like a customer list is an inference by analogy, and it is the reading most practitioners take, but say "by analogy" when you say it. The IASB has a research project on intangibles under way (reported). In practice: the value lenses are management numbers for decisions, not accounting values, and a CFO will expect you to know the difference.

Real world

The asset that was sold in the meeting. In the first index workshop, Sefa proposed adding the repairer invoice lines to the index, "our richest data on repair cost". Maya Okafor, the CDO, pulled up the supply contract: use limited to settling Keelson's own claims. The invoice lines stayed inside, where the economic lens says they earn 1200.0k, and the index was built on the motor claims history alone. Constructed Keelson Mutual example.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Analyst session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Keelson's repairer invoice lines are worth 1200.0k in use and 0.0k on the market. Why the gap?

The data is valuable; the right to pass it on is not Keelson's. The market lens counts lawful buyers only.

2 · The telematics pilot cost 440.0k on the cost lens and earns 15.0k on the economic lens. Which question does that gap answer?

Cost against use value is a keep-or-stop question. The market lens answers a different one.

3 · A board member asks why Keelson's data is not on the balance sheet. What is the accurate answer?

Paragraph 63 names brands, mastheads, publishing titles and customer lists. Applying it to data is a widely taken inference, and saying "by analogy" is the honest version.
